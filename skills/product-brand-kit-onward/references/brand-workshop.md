# Brand Workshop

Find the one thing worth showing first. Use the brief to record the audience's first encounter, what should stay with them, and which real details the work can draw on. Use existing wording, marks, and references when they already answer those questions.

## V01 Name and naming

Record spelling, casing, script, short forms, and joint attribution accurately. Decide whether the first appearance needs a descriptor. For new names, draw from relevant actions, objects, places, or images. Try each materially different candidate next to an avatar, as a poster headline, and in a spoken introduction. Explain what each brings to mind and where it reads best. A working name is sufficient for concept work.

Deliver standard spelling, a small-space short form where useful, and a first-mention example. For a fictional tea-gift brand called Still Hour, use “Still Hour | Tea Gifts” for first contact and “Still Hour” where space is limited. If a shop hosts its tasting, include that shop in the story or a secondary credit when the relationship is confirmed.

## V02 Tagline, contextual copy, and voice

Write down who is speaking to whom at each placement. The main line should be memorable over time. Contextual copy carries the immediate feeling, and a supporting line can tell first-time readers what they need to know. Read each line aloud. Replace an abstraction with a concrete action when it helps. Choose one lead line per composition and preserve approved spelling, punctuation, and allowed line breaks.

| Illustrative line for Still Hour | Placement | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Make time for one good cup.” | Brand introduction | Gives the brand a concrete promise. |
| “This cup is for you.” | The first thing seen inside the gift box | Gives the sender a line addressed directly to the recipient. |
| “Send a cup. Make someone's afternoon.” | Mail-order poster or social keyframe | Supplies the context a first-time viewer needs. |

Deliver copy-ready lines, placements, and a short usage note. Include an example in a real context so the people writing the next version can hear the intended voice.

## V03 Brand story and introduction

Open on an observation or scene, then show what the initiator hopes will happen and how the brand can make that experience possible. An event story can start with why the organizers want people to meet. A space story can start with the first thing people are invited to do there. Frame imagined situations as a vision or invitation, and confirm attributed quotations, identities, and past events.

In the Still Hour example, imagine a friend clearing a spot on a desk for a cup of tea. That scene can carry the idea of an ordinary pause without inventing a founder's story. Deliver a standalone story, a short paragraph for a page or insert, and a sign-off if it is useful. A reader encountering just the short version should still know who the brand is and what it invites them to do.

## V04 Logo

Set the name at its real length. Compare a wordmark, a symbol and type lockup, and a carried-over mark as appropriate. Build an original symbol from a drawable feature such as a gesture, part of a space, or a letterform. Check alignment, visual weight, spacing, and clear space in the first real placement. Sketches or generated studies can support exploration. Deliver an editable vector master, a version with stable appearance for distribution, and transparent PNG where needed.

For example, test a cup and steam symbol alongside Still Hour in a horizontal lockup. An avatar may need a more compact version or the symbol alone, especially where the display name already appears. Shrink the actual artwork before deciding which versions to supply. A tiny descriptor can disappear before the mark itself stops reading.

## V05 Color, type, imagery, and layout

Assign each element a job and show those jobs together in a real composition.

- **Color** Give swatches and screen values for primary, reading, and accent colors. Say what receives the accent. Check text, the mark, and fine lines on light and dark backgrounds.
- **Type and layout** Set the actual name, main line, paragraph, and credit. Record weight, leading, alignment, line breaks, and margins. Include font sources, practical fallbacks, and distribution permissions. Check the fallback against the actual target script.
- **Imagery and supporting graphics** Give the composition one focal point. For photography, specify subject, light, distance, and crop. For illustration and pattern, specify silhouette, line, density, and where it appears. Identify which materials are photographs, original artwork, licensed assets, or concept illustrations.

In the tea-gift example, one dark color can carry the main mark, a light background can support long text, and a small accent can identify the flavor on an insert. A cup-ring pattern on the secondary panels leaves the main panel free for the name and gift line.

## V06 Key visual and adaptation

Use a real first-view placement. Make it clear what viewers see first, which line they read, and where they recognize the name. Use a person, object, original graphic, or typography as the focus. Set the relationship between focus and copy, position the mark and credit, and keep the elements editable on separate layers.

For a fictional weekend reading event, use the action of opening a book as the focus and set “Open a page and tell us why it stayed with you” beside the spine. A vertical poster can show the full action, a square image can crop closer to the book and headline, and a banner can put image and text side by side. Use confirmed details for the event's date and venue.

Check a small thumbnail and real phone size first. Review the required horizontal, square, and vertical crops for faces, artwork, typography, and attribution. Record a practical adaptation decision, such as moving the long story to the post copy while retaining the name and lead line on the square image. For print, check critical strokes and reversed text at the intended size. Supply a standalone export and layered source.

Give the direction board one statement that can be checked in the artwork. For instance, “Read the gift line first, then find the brewing note on opening” can be checked by viewing the lid and first opening together. When the system is ready, include a light and a dark sample with real titles, body copy, and the mark so the next designer can reproduce the choice.
